# Mamaearth Business Model: How Mamaearth Makes Money (2026 Case Study)

Mamaearth started in 2016 with six toxin-free baby products. Ten years later, its parent company, Honasa Consumer Ltd, earned ₹2,392 crore in revenue in FY26 and ₹200 crore in net profit.

In one line, the **Mamaearth business model** is this: Honasa creates and markets brands, partner factories make the products, and the products are sold online and in shops across India. This is called a digital-first, **asset-light** model, which means the company owns very few heavy assets like factories.

This case study covers who Mamaearth sells to, how its model works, how it makes money, how it markets itself, who it competes with, and the ups and downs along the way. Every number is dated and checked against at least two sources, and all Honasa Consumer revenue figures are revenue from operations, as reported in its yearly results.

## About Mamaearth

Mamaearth is an Indian personal care brand based in Gurugram, Haryana. It is the flagship brand of **Honasa Consumer Ltd**, which has been listed on the Indian stock market since November 2023.

The idea came from a problem at home. In 2014, Varun and Ghazal Alagh had their first son and could not find toxin-free baby products in India. Friends and family had to bring products from the US, which was slow and expensive.

Varun had worked as a brand manager at Hindustan Unilever, Diageo and Coca-Cola. In 2016, the couple launched Mamaearth with six baby products, using about ₹30 lakh of their own savings and ₹60 lakh from friends and family. It became Asia's first brand to get MADE SAFE certification, a US label for products made without known harmful chemicals.

## Mamaearth Target Audience

Mamaearth first targeted new mothers who worried about chemicals in baby products. These parents read labels, searched online and trusted other parents' advice.

Soon, the same parents asked for safe products for themselves. So in 2017, Mamaearth moved into adult skin care and hair care. Its core audience became young, online-savvy adults who want natural or "clean" products at a fair price.

Today, Honasa reaches different customers through different brands. Mamaearth speaks to families and young adults, while sister brands target people who want science-led skincare, salon-style hair care or makeup. This matters because one brand cannot be natural, scientific and salon-style all at once. Separate brands let Honasa speak clearly to each group, while sharing one team, one supply chain and one sales network behind the scenes.

## Mamaearth Business Model

The Mamaearth business model rests on six ideas. The first three explain why customers trust it; the last three explain how it grows.

### Connecting With Its Target Audience

Mamaearth was built by parents, for parents. That gave it an honest story that young mothers could relate to. Ghazal Alagh has said the brand grew because mothers recommended it to other mothers, not because of big ad campaigns.

### A Toxin-Free Product Promise

The main promise is **safe, toxin-free** products. Many Mamaearth products are sold as free from parabens, sulphates and SLS, though the exact claim changes from product to product. The MADE SAFE certificate gave anxious parents an outside proof of that promise.

### Lean Innovation Cycle

Honasa watches what customers search for and talk about, then launches products quickly. It launched **122 new products** in 2023, and recently launched products brought in about 9% of revenue in the April to June 2024 quarter. Ingredients like onion, rice water and vitamin C became full product ranges.

### Asset-Light Manufacturing

Honasa's in-house team of about 50 people, led by Ghazal Alagh, creates the formulas. The actual production is done by **contract manufacturers**, which are outside factories paid to make products for the brand.

In 2024, Honasa bought the assets of Cosmogenesis, including an R&D lab with over 4,000 formulations and a small manufacturing unit. So it now owns a little manufacturing capacity, but most production still happens at partner factories. This keeps costs low and lets the company test products in small batches.

### Omnichannel Distribution

Mamaearth began online, selling through its own website and app and on Amazon, Flipkart, Nykaa and FirstCry. It later joined quick commerce apps like Zepto and Swiggy Instamart.

Offline shops then became much more important. According to Honasa's IPO papers, offline sales grew from about 9% of revenue in FY20 to about 29% in FY22. By June 2026, its products reached about **3 lakh retail outlets** across India.

### House of Brands

Honasa does not depend on one brand. Each brand serves a different customer, which spreads risk and widens reach.

| Brand | Focus | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|
| Mamaearth | Natural, toxin-free personal care | Families and young adults |
| The Derma Co | Skincare with active ingredients | People who want science-based skincare |
| Aqualogica | Skincare that adds moisture | Young skincare buyers |
| Dr. Sheth's | Skincare for Indian skin | People with Indian skin concerns |
| BBlunt | Salon-style hair care and styling | People who style their hair |
| Staze | Makeup | Makeup buyers |

Honasa bought BBlunt and Dr. Sheth's in 2022. In FY26, its younger brands grew more than **40%**, faster than Mamaearth itself.

## How Does Mamaearth Earn Money?

Mamaearth earns money by selling products: skin care, hair care, baby care and other personal care items, online and in shops. There is no subscription or service fee; product sales are the business.

Revenue grew from about ₹110 crore in FY20 and ₹460 crore in FY21 to the figures below.

| Financial year | Revenue from operations | Net profit / loss |
|---|---|---|
| FY22 | ₹943 crore | ₹14 crore profit |
| FY23 | ₹1,492 crore | ₹151 crore loss |
| FY24 | ₹1,920 crore | ₹110 crore profit |
| FY25 | ₹2,067 crore | ₹73 crore profit |
| FY26 | ₹2,392 crore | ₹200 crore profit |

The FY23 loss came mainly from writing off about ₹154 crore that Honasa had invested in Momspresso, a content platform. Without that, the business was roughly break-even that year.

The products themselves earn a lot. Honasa's **gross margin**, the money left after paying to make the product, was about 70% in the January to March 2026 quarter. In plain terms, products that sell for ₹100 cost about ₹30 to make.

The big cost is advertising, which took 32.9% of revenue in that quarter. After ads, salaries and other costs, Honasa kept about 8% of FY26 revenue as net profit. One more detail: a change in how the Flipkart group settles payments lowered reported FY26 revenue by about ₹87 crore, without changing profit.

## Mamaearth Marketing Strategy

Marketing is the engine of the Mamaearth business model. The brand moved from word of mouth to celebrities and paid ads as it grew.

### Influencer Marketing and Mom Bloggers

In the early days, there was little money for ads. Ghazal Alagh made friends with mom bloggers, because new mothers trusted each other more than brands.

> "When a mother tells another mother of experience, that has the most trust." — Ghazal Alagh, to Storyboard18

> "At that time, there was no Instagram but bloggers." — Ghazal Alagh, via Business Today

### Free Sampling

Instead of spending on ads, the early team spent on samples. The idea was simple: if people try a good product, they will come back and tell others.

### Celebrity Endorsements

As the brand grew, it added famous faces for wider reach. Shilpa Shetty joined as brand ambassador and investor in 2018. Sara Ali Khan became the face of the hair care range in 2021 and starred in its Onion hair care TV ads.

### Digital and TV Ads

Today, Honasa spends heavily on paid advertising, both online and on TV. In the January to March 2026 quarter, ads took about ₹33 of every ₹100 of revenue. This keeps demand high, but it is also the biggest pressure on profit.

### Cause Marketing: Plant Goodness

Under its Plant Goodness campaign, Mamaearth plants a tree for every order placed on its website and app. It has planted over **8 lakh trees**, which supports its natural, eco-friendly image and gives buyers a feel-good reason to order directly.

## Mamaearth Competitors

Mamaearth competes on two fronts. On one side are other online-first beauty brands, such as WOW Skin Science, Plum, mCaffeine and The Moms Co. These brands also sell "clean" or ingredient-led products to young, online shoppers.

On the other side are large FMCG companies with huge shop networks. The clearest sign of this fight came in January 2025, when Hindustan Unilever agreed to buy 90.5% of Minimalist, a fast-growing skincare brand, at a valuation of about ₹2,955 crore. Big companies are now buying their way into the same customers Mamaearth targets.

## Mamaearth's Ups and Downs

Mamaearth's journey has not been a straight line. In January 2022, Honasa raised $52 million led by Sequoia at a $1.2 billion valuation and became India's first unicorn of 2022. Ghazal Alagh also became a judge on Shark Tank India, which made the brand a household name.

In November 2023, Honasa went public at ₹324 per share, valuing it at about ₹10,500 crore. Many analysts called the price too high, but the IPO was still subscribed about 7.6 times.

The hardest moment came in November 2024. Honasa reported its first quarterly loss, about ₹18.6 crore, because distributors were holding too much stock. Its share price fell **20% in a single day**. The fix, called Project Neev, removed a layer of large wholesalers called super-stockists and appointed direct distributors in India's top 50 cities.

FY26 showed the fix worked. Revenue grew 16%, profit almost tripled to ₹200 crore, and Honasa paid its first-ever dividend of ₹3 per share.

## Conclusion

Mamaearth turned one parent's problem into a ₹2,392 crore company by owning the brand and the customer, and letting partners handle most of the manufacturing. Selling in shops and building new brands are now its main growth drivers, while heavy ad spending remains its biggest challenge. For anyone studying the D2C business model India has built over the last decade, this Mamaearth case study is one of the clearest examples of how an online brand grows into a full FMCG company.
